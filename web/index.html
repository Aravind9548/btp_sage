Why I built this
Every paper I read framed this as a binary — orchestration or choreography, pick one and live with the tradeoff. That never sat right with me. The coordinator is only useful during a rollback, so why pay for it the other 99% of the time? Path D is my answer: stay fully choreographed until something breaks, then elect a leader on the spot and let it schedule compensations against the dependency graph. The numbers below are what came out.
01The Problem

When a distributed saga fails partway through, the steps that already succeeded have to be compensated — refund the card, release the stock, and so on. The catch is that compensations are not independent. Some have to happen in a particular order.

CONSTRAINT   compensate(Payment) must finish BEFORE compensate(Notification) starts

Send the “your order is cancelled” email before the refund actually lands and you have told the customer something that is not true yet. That is the ordering constraint this whole project is about.

02Three Strategiesone of them is mine
Path AOrchestration

A central coordinator walks the completed steps backwards and compensates them one at a time. Ordering is trivially safe because nothing overlaps — which is also why it is slow. Add steps, add latency.

correctslow
Path BChoreography

No coordinator at all. Every service hears the failure and compensates itself immediately. Beautifully decoupled and the fastest option by a wide margin — but it has no way to express ordering, so it breaks the constraint every single time.

0% correctfast
Path DElection + graph

Choreographed while things are fine. The moment a step fails, every service races for a lease; exactly one wins. The winner reads the compensation graph and fires independent steps in parallel, holding back only those that genuinely must wait.
