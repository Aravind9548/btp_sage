Architecture

Four answers to one question

A saga strings a business transaction across services with no shared database transaction tying them together. When one step fails partway through, something has to explicitly undo every step that already succeeded — in the right order. Who decides that order, and when, is the whole design space.

01

The saga and its one real constraint

Four services, run in this order. The benchmark always fails at Inventory — the last step — so every earlier step has completed and needs compensating on every run.

Order→ Notification→ Payment→ Inventory ⚠ fails

The one ordering rule that must hold during rollback: Payment's compensation must finish before Notification's compensation starts — you don't want to tell a customer "refunded" before the refund actually happened. Every architecture below is judged against this single edge, read straight from event-log timestamps after the fact.

02

Four coordination strategies

A — Orchestration

A central coordinator, deciding everything

Runs the forward steps one at a time. On failure, walks backward through completed steps one at a time, waiting for each compensation to finish before starting the next. Simple and predictable — but strictly serial, so cost grows with every step you add.

B — Choreography

No coordinator at all

Every service just reacts to events. One failure event fires, and every completed service compensates itself independently, all at once — with no mechanism for one to wait on another.

D — Election + graph-aware

This project's contribution

On failure, survivors hold a quick lease-based leader election (the same idea as DynamoDB's conditional writes). The winner reads the real compensation dependency graph and dispatches in parallel — except the one edge that genuinely must wait.

D — naive LIFO (ablation)

A control, not a fourth design

Same election as D, but the winner just reverse-order-compensates like A instead of reading the graph. Exists purely to answer: is D's speed from the election, or the scheduler? See the answer →

03

How correctness is judged

One function, verify_rollback, reads the event log after the fact and checks the Payment→Notification edge. It never looks at what a scheduler intended — only what actually happened, in what order.

for edge (Payment → Notification):
  if Notification never compensated  → edge not exercised, vacuously fine
  if Payment never started compensating → nothing for Notification to wait on, fine
  else require: finish(Payment) < start(Notification)

This scoping is what lets one function correctly judge both a full four-step rollback and a partial one — a step that never ran forward has nothing to compensate, and an edge whose target never fired was never at risk.
